# Career Story and Projects

## The Shape of the Career

I started as a designer, kept getting pulled toward the business side of the problems I was designing for, ended up running a marketing function, and am now heading back toward hands-on work. I was curious, I took on things outside what I was hired for, and my managers trusted me with more responsibility. Looking back, it was a common pattern over the fifteen years of my career.

My whole career has been inside marketing organizations. Every one of them was in a high-growth or transformation phase that meant the work constantly crossed functional lines. I have partnered with teams across the organization, from product and engineering to sales, comms, and the executive team. That gave me real exposure to how decisions are made and how to influence their outcomes.

---

## Where the Curiosity Comes From

My mom used to say I never played with toys the way other kids did. I gravitated toward buttons, keys, and locks, anything I could fidget with, take apart, or figure out. Later it was computers and telephones, dismantled purely to see what was inside, with zero idea what I was doing. Sometimes I fixed things and had no idea what I had done.

That instinct never left, and it was a precursor to how I am able to be resourceful and connect what is at hand to provide a solution. I am not a master of any one discipline and I do not pretend to be. What I am good at is walking into something unfamiliar and picking it up quickly, because I go for the underlying logic rather than the surface. Every field has a system running underneath it. Once I can see the patterns, I can navigate within them. Design, conversion funnels, marketing automation, messaging frameworks, PR, and lately AI tooling are all the same instinct pointed at different objects. My CliftonStrengths results put Input and Ideation in my top five, which is the assessment's way of saying I collect a lot and I connect it.

As I have gotten older the curiosity has extended to people too. I have been told I am easy to talk to, that I ask good questions, that people tend to open up around me. I think that is just what happens when you are actually interested in the person in front of you rather than waiting for your turn to talk.

## URBANARA: Owning the Site End to End (2012–2016)

I moved to Berlin in 2012 and for a while kept freelancing for clients back in Los Angeles and New York while I looked for something permanent here. What I found was URBANARA, a Berlin homewares brand selling direct to consumers, and I joined in February 2012 as Senior Web and UX Designer. I stayed four years and two months.

I joined as the sole web designer, working alongside the senior graphic designer who handled visual design, print, packaging, and illustration. Since we were an online brand, everything that appeared on the site was my domain. I created imagery, built and optimized landing pages, published site content, and every week I hand-built the newsletter. It meant I learned the site from the inside out, every template and every quirk.

This meant that I took full ownership of the web experience and was involved in any project that touched the website. It also meant that I was involved with the online marketing teams driving traffic to the website. We worked to optimize user journeys from paid and organic sources all the way from landing pages to checkout. I learned to defend my design decisions not based on feeling but on best practice research and data.

It was a startup of twenty people when I joined, so the org chart was flat. On paper, everyone laddered up to one of the three co-founders. My manager was the Director of Product and Marketing. Product in her case meant the physical product, so she ran buying, sourcing, and owned all of brand and marketing. Four years of unbroken access to the person making the decisions is rare, and it is a large part of why I got as much room to operate as I did.

At URBANARA as we grew we hired more designers to join the team and I was able to learn what mentoring and coaching meant. When we hired a second person onto web, they did not report to me directly on paper, but I became their go-to person as a lead designer, so I prioritized work, reviewed and gave feedback on outputs, and did the coaching. I handed over content production, site management, and the newsletter, which freed me up for the UX and UI work on the site itself, plus whatever marketing needed on the digital side.

The senior graphic designer took on someone under her, and when she left I picked up her report alongside the web designer. From there the team grew again, another designer and then another. Eventually the company brought in someone to own brand and visual design properly, managing the team, the photoshoots, and the brand work. That was the point where I narrowed down to the website and stopped being involved in the graphic and brand side day to day, though I still pulled designers in whenever a web project needed support. For the first few years, though, I had a hand in nearly all of it.

Then came the full website redesign, and I owned that project outright. I set the direction, briefed and managed the external agency, took their designs back in, and worked directly with our developers through implementation. It was the first time I was accountable for the outcome rather than a deliverable. It also put me in every conversation I had previously been downstream of: scope with the agency, positioning with the marketing director, feasibility with the developers. I went from receiving the brief to writing it, and I found I cared more about whether the thing worked than whether it looked good.

In October 2013 I was promoted into the Ecommerce Manager role, which actually turned out to be the product owner for the website. I defined the site roadmap and vision, owned the onsite customer journey, and ran conversion optimization and A/B testing strategy, directing a two-person team of site managers on execution and priorities. The work itself was optimizing product pages, listing pages, the homepage, and the checkout flow through data-driven testing and user research, and acting as the bridge between leadership, design, and development.

Owning conversion means you cannot fall back on taste. The test wins or it does not, and after four years of that I stopped being precious about my own ideas. This is also where I learned to work like an owner rather than a contributor: roadmap, hypotheses, tradeoffs, and being on the hook for the number. To be precise, that was ownership of a website and an ecommerce funnel inside a marketing team, not product management of a software product.

## Cloudflare: First Web Hire, Through the IPO (2019–2022)

I joined Cloudflare in April 2019 as Lead UX Designer for the marketing web experience, starting in San Francisco and later moving back to Berlin while staying with the company. Three years in total, spanning the IPO and the company's growth into the $1B+ revenue stage.

I was the first dedicated web and UX hire for cloudflare.com, which explains most of what the role involved. There was no design program to join, so I built the foundations: workflows, processes, methodologies. A lot of the first year went on shifting how the organization thought about design, from something you call in at the end to make things look right, to something that shapes what gets built. That took a long time and does not show up in a portfolio, but it is the work I am most pleased with.

The visible output of that first year was the homepage redesign, the careers site refresh, and the Project Galileo pages, plus partnering with senior executive leadership on the cloudflare.net launch supporting the company's IPO debut. Underneath that I was optimizing the core conversion pages, customer stories, enterprise, and onboarding, and building landing pages for events and product launches, proving impact through user testing and analytics rather than argument.

In April 2020 I moved into the Manager role for the same team, which happened to be the month the world went remote. I built and led a two-person team of UX/UI designers, partnering with marketing, product management, and engineering to improve visitor experience and optimize conversion paths through experimentation and testing. The strategic projects were a complete site refresh, translating the brand identity and design system across the entire website while restructuring and redesigning the navigation, and a run at the key conversion pages and journeys, homepage, pricing, navigation, and the recommendation engine, which delivered a 25% increase in conversion rate.

The other half of the management job was structural: cross-functional partnerships across the organization, shared design systems and research practices, less design debt, faster delivery.

Two things I took from Cloudflare. The first is what managing teaches you about why decisions get made the way they do: what business pressures shape a roadmap, how to move a cross-functional team, how much pipeline marketing has to contribute to hit the number.

The second is how B2B buying actually works. You are never talking to one person. A buying committee means several people need to understand something complex in their own way and on their own timeline, so the job is making complicated things clear and showing up long before anyone is ready to decide.

## Lucanet: Running the Function (2022–2025)

I joined Lucanet in Berlin in May 2022 as Team Lead for Content and Creation and left in August 2025 as Head of Corporate Marketing. Three years and four months, three roles. Lucanet makes financial software, an integrated platform for finance and tax teams, and while I was there it went through a repositioning and a hyper-growth phase backed by private equity firm Hg Capital.

**Team Lead, Content and Creation (May 2022 to August 2023).** I inherited a team of seven and grew it to nine: content, design, and translation, sitting together. We ran a content engine across key European and Asian markets, promoting a consistent brand voice while adapting the message for each audience.

Most of what I did that first year was understanding the business and team needs and ways of working. Optimizing workflows and processes so the team spent attention in the right places, and building agency partnerships so we could scale capacity when demand spiked instead of burning people out, including support for company-owned and regional events.

**Senior Global Digital Experience Manager (August 2023 to September 2024).** This is the move where the web stopped being an output and became a platform. Lucanet's web presence had been an execution function, a place where things got published after being decided elsewhere. The remit was to turn it into something strategic that drove brand visibility, customer education, and lead generation across markets.

I led the brand refresh RFP and the agency selection, working with senior and executive leadership on direction and feedback for the positioning and identity work. Then I translated that refresh into the digital experience through a full website redesign and a replatform onto Adobe Experience Manager, which came out at 10% year on year traffic growth and a 3% conversion rate. And we consolidated the country-specific domains into a single .com property supporting six languages, which delivered significant SEO gains and meant the brand finally looked like one company across its core markets.

**Head of Corporate Marketing (September 2024 to August 2025).** I stepped up to lead brand, design, content, digital experience, and corporate communications, reporting to the VP of Marketing, who reported to the CRO. There was no CMO. I ran a €1.2M budget across a 13-person multi-functional team, through the middle of the repositioning and the growth phase, with a real contribution toward Lucanet's ARR milestone (€100M → €200M).

The shift here was from owning the web to owning reputation, which was a real step up in scope. On communications I directed our PR agency engagements and the external communications strategy, which secured over 4M media reach in publications including Bloomberg, CFO.com, Handelsblatt, and Treasury Today, lifted share of voice by 30%, and got our executives into conversations worth being in. I championed the content marketing strategy behind our flagship industry report, and gave direction on the analyst reports we produced with BARC and IDC, which the team co-authored. The team launched the corporate podcast and coordinated executive speaking engagements. The social side, including the LinkedIn growth to over 50K followers, was a big milestone. This was the outcome of moving from a reactive function to building a center of excellence that drove its strategy tied to business outcomes.

I also coordinated the external communications around the company's M&A activity: partner and customer outreach, press releases, and the post-merger brand integrations.

Alongside that I guided the brand evolution that followed the initial refresh, working with leadership to refine positioning and messaging, and guided the team through executing the largest customer and partner conference the company had run, over 300 attendees, designed to drive expansion. On the digital side the work carried on: 20% year on year growth in organic traffic through SEO, platform enhancements, and content strategy alignment.

Most of that has been learning and building with AI as a development partner. I have applications running on a Raspberry Pi at home, because I wanted to understand how the tools work. Same instinct as taking apart telephones as a kid.

The part I have found most useful is the consulting framing, working out whether a problem needs AI at all and which approach actually fits, instead of reaching for the biggest tool available. Kelp farming, ocean restoration, and the businesses being built around them. It is the direction that is pulling on me the most.
